Can public procurement be harmonised without sacrificing the autonomy of the governments responsible for implementing it?

Imagine a construction company registered in Lagos that wants to bid for public contracts in Abuja, Kaduna, Enugu and Rivers State. Despite operating within the same country, the company may have to navigate different procurement portals, registration requirements, bidding documents and administrative procedures before it can effectively compete across these jurisdictions.

Now imagine extending that experience across 27 different countries.

This is, to some extent, the challenge the European Union is attempting to address through its proposed Public Procurement Act. However, an interesting disagreement is emerging over how that objective should be achieved.

On 23 November 2026, MLex reported that a substantial group of EU member states was pushing back against the European Commission's proposal to introduce a directly applicable public procurement regulation. These countries would rather have a directive that allows national governments greater discretion over implementation.

For a market in which public authorities spend approximately €2.5 trillion annually on procurement, representing about 15% of the EU's combined GDP, this is hardly an insignificant disagreement.

It raises a fundamental question about public procurement reform: how much uniformity is desirable, and at what point does harmonisation begin to undermine legitimate institutional autonomy?

A regulation or a directive? Why the distinction matters

On 9 September 2026, the European Commission presented its proposed Public Procurement Act, intended to consolidate and replace the three principal procurement directives adopted in 2014, covering general public procurement, utilities procurement and concession contracts.

The Commission proposes to replace these directives with a single regulation.

To the average contractor, the distinction between a regulation and a directive may sound like something best left to lawyers. However, the practical implications are considerable.

Under Article 288 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, a directive binds member states to achieve prescribed outcomes while allowing national authorities to determine how those outcomes are incorporated into their domestic legal systems. A regulation, on the other hand, is binding in its entirety and directly applicable across member states.

In simple terms, a directive tells governments where they need to get to, while leaving them some latitude in determining how to get there. A regulation prescribes more of the common legal framework within which that journey takes place.

The European Commission's proposal is understandable. If Europe is to function as a genuinely integrated market, why should businesses face avoidable differences in procurement requirements simply because a potential government customer is located across a national border?

However, the concerns of the member states are equally worth examining.

The attraction of a common procurement rulebook

One of the most interesting aspects of the proposed Act is the ambition to interconnect national electronic procurement systems into an integrated European procurement marketplace.

Under the proposed framework, businesses would be able to participate in procurement opportunities across the EU through interconnected platforms, while the application of the 'once-only' principle would reduce the need to repeatedly submit information already available within the system.

Anyone familiar with public procurement in developing economies will immediately appreciate the significance of this.

Consider a small business that wants to supply medical consumables to public hospitals across several jurisdictions. Before it even begins competing on price, quality or delivery capacity, it may have to spend considerable time and resources satisfying multiple administrative requirements, many of which establish essentially the same facts.

The larger companies can afford dedicated bid management teams to navigate these processes. Smaller businesses often cannot.

We frequently talk about increasing SME participation in public procurement through preferences, reserved contracts and other affirmative measures. Perhaps one of the most effective interventions is simply to make it less expensive and less cumbersome for these businesses to participate in the first place.

Harmonisation can help achieve this. It can also facilitate procurement benchmarking, improve the comparability of government expenditure, strengthen transparency and create a more competitive supplier market.

But does achieving these objectives necessarily require identical procurement laws?

When uniformity becomes a problem

Public procurement does not operate in isolation. It exists within a wider ecosystem of budgeting, public financial management, administrative law, industrial policy, institutional capacity and political accountability.

These conditions are not necessarily identical across jurisdictions.

The procurement of medical equipment for a sophisticated urban hospital, for instance, may require an approach considerably different from the procurement of basic healthcare supplies for a network of rural health centres. Market availability, logistics, maintenance capacity and even the number of qualified suppliers may differ significantly.

A common procurement framework must be sufficiently flexible to accommodate such realities.

Having spent a significant part of my professional career working on public procurement legislation, regulations and their implementation, I have come to appreciate that the effectiveness of a procurement law is not necessarily determined by how elaborate or internationally aligned its provisions are.

It is equally determined by whether the institutions responsible for implementing those provisions possess the capacity, resources and administrative arrangements required to make them work.

This is where the European debate becomes particularly interesting. A directly applicable regulation can reduce inconsistencies in national legislation, but it cannot automatically eliminate differences in institutional capacity, administrative culture or procurement outcomes.

Uniform legislation does not guarantee uniform performance.

There is also a distinction worth making between the discretion of governments to design their procurement systems and the discretion of individual procurement officials to make procurement decisions. These are two different forms of discretion, and reducing one does not necessarily address the risks associated with the other.

What can Nigeria learn from Europe?

Nigeria provides an interesting, albeit constitutionally different, context in which to examine this debate.

At the federal level, we have the Public Procurement Act 2007. Several states have subsequently developed their own procurement laws, regulatory institutions and administrative frameworks. Lagos State, for instance, operates under its Public Procurement Law 2021.

This decentralised arrangement creates opportunities for states to innovate, develop policies suited to their economic circumstances and experiment with different approaches to procurement reform.

A state may decide to leverage public procurement to improve the economic participation of women-owned businesses. Another may prioritise local manufacturing, agricultural value chains or the development of its technology ecosystem.

Such flexibility can be valuable.

However, decentralisation also raises some uncomfortable questions.

Why should a contractor who has already undergone a credible verification process in one jurisdiction be required to reproduce substantially the same documentation in another? Why should procurement data across governments be so difficult to aggregate and compare? Why should businesses have to navigate unnecessarily different electronic systems to access opportunities funded by public resources within the same country?

Subject to the necessary legal, technical and data protection arrangements, Nigeria could explore greater interoperability between procurement systems, mutual recognition of certain supplier credentials, common procurement data standards and a central discovery portal through which businesses can identify government contracting opportunities across participating jurisdictions.

None of these interventions necessarily requires abolishing state procurement laws or establishing a single national procurement regulator.

We can harmonise systems without necessarily centralising institutions.

Beyond Nigeria: an opportunity for Africa

The conversation becomes even more relevant within the context of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA).

As African countries pursue greater economic integration, it is worth considering the role that public procurement could play in expanding commercial opportunities for African businesses.

Indeed, ECOWAS has previously initiated work towards harmonising procurement systems and practices across its member states. The idea of regional procurement harmonisation is therefore not entirely new to our continent.

Imagine a Nigerian technology company being able to identify and compete for suitable government contracts in Ghana, Kenya or Rwanda without encountering unnecessarily repetitive administrative barriers.

Imagine African governments adopting compatible procurement data standards that allow researchers, investors, development institutions and citizens to compare public expenditure and contracting outcomes across countries.

The economic possibilities are considerable. However, opening public procurement markets must also be carefully balanced against legitimate domestic development objectives, local content requirements, applicable trade commitments and the need to protect emerging industries.

Africa need not replicate the European model. It can, however, learn from the difficulties Europe is currently confronting.

Perhaps we are asking the wrong question

Much of the discussion about procurement harmonisation tends to focus on whether jurisdictions should adopt the same rules.

I believe a more useful starting point is to determine which aspects of public procurement actually need to be uniform and which aspects benefit from local flexibility.

Common standards for transparency, supplier verification, procurement data, competition, integrity and accountability can coexist with different institutional arrangements and developmental priorities.

Governments can retain the flexibility to pursue legitimate policy objectives while ensuring that such flexibility does not become a justification for unnecessary bureaucracy, protectionism or arbitrary decision-making.

As the European Union continues to negotiate its proposed Public Procurement Act, procurement practitioners and policymakers elsewhere should pay close attention.

The debate is not simply about a regulation or a directive. It is about the architecture of public procurement governance and the distribution of authority within increasingly integrated economies.

After all, the ultimate objective of procurement reform should not be to ensure that every government procures in exactly the same way. It should be to ensure that every government procures well.

And there is a considerable difference between the two.
