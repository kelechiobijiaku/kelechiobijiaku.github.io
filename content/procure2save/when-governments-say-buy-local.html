Something interesting is happening in international public procurement, and I think we should be paying closer attention to it, particularly here in Africa.

For years, the conversation around public procurement has largely centred on transparency, competition, fairness and value for money. Open up the market, allow qualified suppliers to compete, evaluate their bids fairly and award contracts in accordance with established rules.

Fair enough.

But governments are increasingly asking another question. Why should billions in public expenditure not also be used to strengthen domestic industries, protect jobs and build national economic capacity?

And interestingly, this conversation is no longer confined to developing economies.

On 9 September 2026, the European Commission unveiled a proposal for a new Public Procurement Act. The proposed legislation would replace the existing procurement directives with a single regulation, simplify procurement procedures and introduce a stronger European preference framework.

In other words, Europe is considering how to use its enormous public procurement market more deliberately to support European businesses and industries.

Now, here's where things get interesting.

The proposal goes beyond merely encouraging governments to purchase European products. It also seeks to integrate environmental sustainability, innovation, security and supply-chain resilience into procurement decisions.

Under the proposed framework, quality-related criteria would generally account for at least 30% of evaluation weighting, rising to 50% for labour-intensive contracts, subject to an alternative approach where contracting authorities explain how quality is otherwise assured.

There is also a proposal for a connected digital procurement marketplace across the European Union.

Of course, this is still proposed legislation. It requires approval by the European Parliament and Council, with the institutions working towards adoption by the end of 2027.

But the direction is quite revealing.

Public procurement is increasingly being treated not simply as an administrative mechanism for purchasing goods and services, but as an instrument of economic and industrial policy.

And that brings me to Nigeria.

Our Nigeria First policy similarly seeks to prioritise Nigerian-made goods and capable indigenous businesses in government procurement. In his 2026 budget address, President Tinubu emphasised the policy's role in supporting local industries, creating employment and reducing dependence on imports.

Only last week, on 22 October, the Presidency again highlighted this policy while discussing opportunities for indigenous engineering companies in Nigeria's oil and gas industry.

So, in a sense, Nigeria and Europe are confronting a similar policy question, although from very different economic positions.

How do we use government purchasing power to develop our domestic economies without undermining competition, quality and efficiency?

I find this particularly interesting because public procurement has always involved more than simply purchasing things.

When a government awards a contract for the construction of a hospital, for instance, it is purchasing infrastructure. But that expenditure can also create employment, support local manufacturers, develop technical expertise and strengthen domestic supply chains.

The question is whether the procurement system is deliberately designed to achieve these additional benefits.

However, there's a catch.

Local preference policies can become counterproductive if they are poorly implemented.

Imagine a government restricting competition in favour of domestic suppliers without first establishing whether those suppliers possess the necessary technical capacity, financial strength or production capability.

What happens when delivery fails?

Or when protected suppliers become less competitive because they know that government contracts are practically guaranteed?

We could end up protecting inefficiency rather than promoting industrial development.

And I think this is where procurement professionals, commercial lawyers and policymakers have an important responsibility.

A properly designed local content policy should not simply favour domestic businesses. It should help them become genuinely competitive.

That means developing transparent eligibility criteria, verifying local value addition, encouraging technology transfer, strengthening supplier development programmes and measuring actual contract performance.

It also means distinguishing between a genuinely indigenous manufacturer and a company that merely imports finished products, changes the packaging and presents them as locally manufactured.

Both may be legally registered domestic businesses, but their contributions to industrial development are hardly the same.

There are commercial law implications too.

Local content requirements must be properly reflected in tender documents and contractual obligations. Otherwise, how do we enforce commitments relating to local employment, technology transfer or domestic sourcing?

And where international trade commitments apply, procurement preferences must be carefully structured to remain legally defensible.

I would also argue that African countries should think beyond their individual national markets.

If every African government pursues local preference without considering regional supply chains, we risk creating fragmented markets that are too small to support competitive industries.

Perhaps there is room for a more coordinated African approach, where domestic industrial development and regional trade reinforce rather than undermine each other.

After all, a Nigerian manufacturer that becomes competitive enough to supply public institutions in Ghana, Kenya or Rwanda has achieved something considerably more valuable than merely securing preferential access to contracts at home.

The European proposal has reminded me of something important.

Public procurement is not just about how governments spend money. It is also about what those spending decisions make possible.

The challenge is ensuring that our pursuit of economic sovereignty does not become an excuse for inefficiency, protectionism or reduced accountability.

Because buying local should ultimately help us build businesses capable of competing globally.

Otherwise, what exactly are we protecting?
