There's something about the lowest bid that makes it particularly attractive.

For a procurement officer, it can be reassuring. For an accounting officer, it may appear financially prudent. And for an auditor reviewing the procurement process several months later, the decision might seem perfectly reasonable.

After all, if one contractor offers to execute a project for ₦800 million while another quotes ₦950 million, why should government spend an additional ₦150 million?

It's a fair question. But here's another question we don't ask often enough.

What if the ₦800 million contract eventually costs ₦1.3 billion? And what if the contractor who quoted ₦950 million could have delivered the same project within budget, on schedule and to the required standard?

Suddenly, the cheapest bid doesn't look particularly cheap anymore.

This is one of the more interesting contradictions in public procurement. We spend considerable time establishing procedures to ensure that government obtains competitive prices. Yet, sometimes, we pay insufficient attention to what happens after the contract has been awarded. And that is where the real cost of procurement often emerges.

The difference between price and value

Let me clarify something. There is absolutely nothing wrong with awarding a contract to the lowest bidder.

In fact, where bids satisfy the applicable responsiveness, qualification and evaluation requirements, selecting the lowest evaluated responsive bid can be entirely appropriate. The problem arises when we confuse the lowest quoted price with the lowest overall cost. They are not necessarily the same thing.

Consider a contractor who submits an unusually low bid to secure a government project. Six months into implementation, the contractor begins experiencing cash-flow difficulties. Materials become more expensive. Progress slows. Requests for variations start arriving.

Eventually, the project requires additional funding, an extension of time or, in particularly unfortunate circumstances, termination and reprocurement.

The original contract price may have been competitive. The eventual procurement outcome certainly wasn't.

This is why international procurement practice emphasises value for money, appropriate evaluation methodologies and consideration of costs over the relevant life cycle. The World Bank's Procurement Regulations for IPF Borrowers, for example, recognise that value for money involves more than simply obtaining the lowest initial price. The objective is to achieve an appropriate balance of quality, cost and other relevant considerations.

But procurement officers are not fortune-tellers

One difficulty with this conversation is that procurement professionals cannot predict every future development. Exchange rates fluctuate. Inflation changes. Supply chains become disrupted. Governments sometimes delay payments.

Even a perfectly capable contractor can encounter difficulties that were not reasonably foreseeable when the contract was awarded.

So, the solution cannot simply be to reject every unusually low bid or automatically select a more expensive contractor. That would create another problem entirely.

Instead, procurement institutions need evaluation systems capable of distinguishing between genuinely competitive pricing and pricing that creates unacceptable delivery risks. Depending on the applicable procurement rules and the nature of the contract, this may involve examining price realism, technical methodology, financial capacity, implementation schedules and life-cycle costs.

The important point is that these considerations must be incorporated transparently into the procurement process, rather than introduced arbitrarily after bids have been received. Procurement discretion must remain accountable.

The contract award is not the finish line

There is another dimension to this issue that deserves attention. Even where the right contractor is selected through a properly conducted procurement process, value for money can still disappear during contract implementation.

Poorly drafted contracts, inadequate supervision, weak performance indicators and uncontrolled variations can undermine an otherwise successful procurement exercise. This is why I believe contract management deserves significantly more institutional attention.

Think about it. An organisation may spend months developing procurement plans, preparing tender documents, evaluating bids and obtaining the necessary approvals. Yet, once the contract is signed, the same level of discipline is not always sustained.

Contract management is sometimes treated as a largely administrative exercise involving payment certificates, progress reports and correspondence. But effective contract management should be much more than that.

It should involve continuous monitoring of contractual obligations, early identification of emerging risks, verification of deliverables, management of variations and timely intervention when performance begins to deteriorate. The objective is not merely to establish whether the contractor has complied with the agreement. It is to ensure that the organisation actually receives the value it contracted to obtain.

What this means for Nigeria and other developing economies

For countries facing significant infrastructure deficits and competing demands on public resources, the consequences of poor procurement outcomes are particularly serious. Every abandoned project, avoidable cost escalation or poorly performing contract represents resources that could have been deployed elsewhere.

This makes the relationship between procurement evaluation and contract management especially important. We need procurement systems that encourage genuine competition without rewarding commercially unrealistic promises.

We also need institutions that understand that contract administration is not something to worry about only when disputes arise. It begins with the way the procurement itself is designed.

Clear specifications, realistic delivery timelines, appropriate risk allocation, measurable performance standards and well-considered payment structures can all influence whether a contract succeeds. And procurement professionals should be involved in these conversations from the beginning.

A final thought

Perhaps we need to become a little less impressed by how much money a procurement exercise appears to have saved at the point of contract award. A lower contract price is certainly worth celebrating. But the more meaningful question is whether those savings survive implementation.

Did the contractor deliver? Was the project completed within the agreed parameters? Did the organisation receive the required quality? And, ultimately, was public money well spent?

Because the true cost of procurement is not always the figure written on the contract award letter. Sometimes, it is the amount we eventually pay to correct the consequences of getting the procurement wrong.

And that is why the cheapest bid can occasionally become the most expensive contract.
