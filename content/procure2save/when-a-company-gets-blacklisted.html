Imagine a company spending years building its contracting business. It acquires equipment, hires capable people, delivers projects and earns the confidence of its clients.

Then one procurement investigation changes the conversation.

Suddenly, future tenders are in doubt. A prospective joint-venture partner wants explanations. The compliance team is looking through old bid documents, and management is asking how long the company might be excluded from government business.

The immediate sanction may not be the most expensive part. The real cost could be the contracts the company will never get the chance to compete for.

That is why I think supplier debarment deserves more attention than it usually receives.

Nigeria has just made the conversation particularly relevant.

Nigeria's new enforcement push

In October 2026, Nigeria's Bureau of Public Procurement announced the enforcement of a national guideline under which contractors, consultants and service providers may face exclusion from federal procurement for three to five years for specified misconduct.

The reported grounds include bribery, falsified documents, fraudulent conduct and wilful or persistent failure to perform contractual obligations. A prior sanction by a multilateral development institution may also be considered.

What interests me is the connection between integrity and actual contract performance. Debarment is not only about a corrupt bidding process. A contractor that repeatedly fails to deliver may also create a serious risk for public expenditure.

But there is an important legal distinction. Section 58(6) of Nigeria's Public Procurement Act 2007 separately prescribes, upon conviction of a corporate offender, debarment for not less than five years together with a financial penalty. That statutory consequence should not be casually confused with the administrative periods described in the new guideline.

Different legal routes require careful attention to their respective conditions and safeguards.

The UK's risk-based approach

In the United Kingdom, the Procurement Act 2023 has established a central debarment framework. The Debarment Review Service, now within the Government Commercial Agency, investigates suppliers for possible inclusion on the published list; the final decision rests with a Minister.

The distinction between mandatory and discretionary exclusion matters. Some listed suppliers must generally be excluded from covered procurements; in other cases, authorities retain discretion, subject to proper assessment and justification.

Perhaps the more interesting point is the philosophy behind the system. The UK's guidance treats debarment as a measure for managing continuing or recurring supplier risk, rather than simply a punishment for historical wrongdoing.

Has the company addressed the conduct? Have its controls improved? Is the risk likely to recur?

Those are more useful questions than merely asking whether something went wrong five years ago.

When misconduct crosses borders

The World Bank's list of ineligible firms and individuals shows why this issue is no longer confined to one jurisdiction.

Under the cross-debarment arrangements among participating multilateral development banks, certain sanctions may be recognised by other institutions, subject to the agreement's rules. A company sanctioned in one development-finance setting can therefore face consequences for opportunities elsewhere.

That does not mean every allegation or every national blacklisting automatically produces a worldwide ban. The legal basis and scope of each sanction still matter.

Nevertheless, for suppliers bidding on donor-funded infrastructure, energy, technology or consulting projects, the commercial implications are obvious.

Compliance is part of business strategy

There is a practical lesson here for both sides of the procurement table.

Suppliers need to know what their agents promise, what their subcontractors do and whether the representations in their bids can actually be supported. They should document conflicts of interest, maintain reliable performance records and investigate credible complaints before those complaints become a crisis.

Procurement authorities, meanwhile, need more than a spreadsheet of blacklisted names. They need dependable evidence, clear procedures, opportunities for suppliers to respond, proportionate decisions and a credible route for review or rehabilitation where the applicable rules permit it.

Otherwise, a safeguard intended to protect public money could become a mechanism for arbitrary exclusion.

This is particularly important in African procurement markets, where supplier capacity is sometimes limited and government remains a major purchaser. Excluding a supplier can protect the public interest, but doing so carelessly can also reduce competition and invite litigation.

One final thought

We often treat procurement compliance as a legal department's problem, something to address after a complaint arrives.

I see it differently.

A company's eligibility to compete for future business is itself a valuable commercial asset. Protecting that asset requires honest bidding, reliable delivery, sound internal controls and management that takes warning signs seriously.

A contract won through misconduct may look profitable today. The opportunities lost tomorrow could make it the most expensive contract the company ever secured.

And that is a cost no sensible business should ignore.
