Why government contracts could do more for women, persons with disabilities and the communities that fund them

Something about the way we discuss domestic preference in Nigerian public procurement has always interested me.

The conventional argument is that government should give some advantage to Nigerian businesses when they compete against foreign suppliers. That makes sense in appropriate circumstances, particularly where international competitive bidding is involved. But the reality is that a significant proportion of procurement opportunities at the state and local government levels are already contested among Nigerian businesses.

So, what exactly are we achieving by discussing domestic preference almost exclusively in terms of Nigerian versus foreign suppliers?

Perhaps we should be asking a different question: Can public procurement be used more deliberately to expand economic opportunities for people and communities that have historically struggled to access them?

I think this is a conversation Nigeria needs to have.

Preference doesn't always mean favouritism

In February 2026, Nigeria's Bureau of Public Procurement announced a collaborative initiative with the International Trade Centre and Korea's Public Procurement Service to strengthen understanding of affirmative procurement. The proposed national policy is intended to improve opportunities for groups including women, young people and persons with disabilities.

This is an encouraging development, particularly because affirmative procurement is no longer an entirely unfamiliar concept in Nigeria.

Kaduna has introduced measures to expand procurement opportunities for women-owned businesses, while Lagos announced the approval of a gender-responsive procurement framework in 2025, including a minimum allocation of 10% of eligible contracts to women-owned enterprises.

These initiatives reflect an important recognition: treating everyone identically in a procurement process does not necessarily mean everyone has an equal opportunity to compete.

Consider a woman-owned business with the technical competence to execute a government contract but limited access to finance, previous government contracts or the extensive documentation sometimes demanded during qualification. Or an entrepreneur with a disability who encounters inaccessible procurement platforms and administrative procedures.

Removing those barriers, simplifying appropriate requirements and providing targeted opportunities may achieve more than simply announcing that procurement is open to everyone.

And where the applicable law permits, a modest preference margin or carefully designed reservation can help make participation more meaningful.

What if states took this further?

Now consider another possibility.

A state government awards billions of naira in contracts annually. Yet many of the businesses executing those contracts may have little economic presence in the communities where the projects are located.

Could the government design procurement incentives that encourage bidders to establish genuine local operations, employ residents, train young people or source suitable materials from businesses within the state?

For example, imagine a state introducing a modest, transparently disclosed evaluation advantage for qualified contractors that commit to employing a defined proportion of residents on a labour-intensive project. Alternatively, a contractor could earn recognition for establishing a functioning local service centre, providing apprenticeships or subcontracting appropriate portions of work to capable local SMEs.

These are not necessarily the same as excluding businesses from other states. A company headquartered in Abuja could still compete for a contract in Lagos, employ Lagos residents and establish the facilities required to deliver the project effectively.

The emphasis would be on measurable economic contribution rather than ancestry.

And there is a potentially important distinction here. A preference based on genuine local employment or service-delivery capacity may be easier to justify than one based simply on whether the business owner is considered an indigene.

The constitutional question cannot be ignored

Nigeria's Constitution protects citizens against discrimination on grounds including ethnic group, community and place of origin. Section 42 is particularly relevant, while Section 15 emphasises national integration and the need to promote a sense of belonging among Nigerians.

Consequently, any proposal to reserve public contracts exclusively for state indigenes would raise serious constitutional questions.

This is especially important in a country where conversations about indigeneity, residence and ethnic identity can become politically sensitive.

But there is room for a more thoughtful policy discussion.

Rather than asking where a company's owners originally come from, governments could examine whether the business contributes to the economy of the state through employment, skills development, genuine operational presence and local supply-chain participation.

Even these measures would require a proper legal basis, objective criteria and careful assessment of their effects on competition. A local-office requirement, for instance, should not become an artificial barrier designed to favour established contractors.

A better approach might permit successful bidders to establish the necessary presence after award, where that is sufficient for effective contract performance.

The distinction is subtle, but important.

Government can seek to develop its local economy without turning public procurement into an instrument of ethnic exclusion.

But who pays for the preference?

There is, of course, another side to this discussion.

Every procurement preference has a potential cost. If government accepts a higher evaluated price to achieve a social or economic objective, that additional expenditure must be justified.

A preference should therefore be modest, legally authorised, transparently disclosed and connected to measurable public benefits. It should never excuse poor technical competence, unacceptable quality or failure to deliver.

There is also the familiar risk of fronting. A business could claim to be women-owned, disability-inclusive or locally established without the intended beneficiaries exercising genuine ownership, control or receiving meaningful economic benefits.

Verification, beneficial-ownership checks, contract monitoring and periodic evaluation would be essential.

Perhaps most importantly, governments should measure outcomes rather than merely count preferential awards. How many sustainable jobs were created? Did participating businesses grow? Were contracts successfully completed? Did the intervention improve access without substantially undermining competition?

Those questions should determine whether a preference continues, changes or ends.

A different way of looking at value for money

We often measure procurement success through the price paid and the quality of goods, works or services delivered. Those considerations remain fundamental.

But public expenditure can also generate wider economic benefits when procurement policies are carefully designed.

A contract that delivers a functioning public facility while developing local technical capacity, expanding opportunities for women-owned enterprises or creating sustainable employment may produce benefits that extend beyond the immediate project.

That does not mean every social objective belongs in every tender. Procurement must remain competitive, accountable and commercially sensible.

It does suggest, however, that governments should think more deliberately about the economic outcomes their purchasing decisions can produce.

Nigeria's emerging affirmative procurement conversation offers an opportunity to do exactly that.

Perhaps the future of domestic preference should not be limited to protecting Nigerian businesses from foreign competition. It could also involve creating fairer opportunities within Nigeria itself.

And for state governments, the question may be even more immediate.

When we spend public money, are we simply purchasing goods and services, or are we also building the economic capacity of the people and communities we serve?

I think we can do both, provided we are thoughtful about how we go about it.
